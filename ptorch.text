\begin{abstract}
Projection-based optimization offers a gradient-free alternative to conventional gradient-based training, but existing frameworks suffer from high computational costs, poor scaling with depth, and limited integration with modern deep learning infrastructure. The motivation for this line of research is the risk of paradigm lock-in: the overwhelming focus on gradient-based training may lead the community to overlook alternative learning frameworks that, given comparable research investment, could ultimately prove competitive or superior. We introduce $\mathcal{P}$Torch, a projection-based framework that builds directly on top of PyTorch's autograd engine, eliminating the overhead of prior approaches. We further improve performance through nonlinear relaxation and memory-efficient projections, making the method competitive with gradient-based baselines on shallow MLPs for MNIST and CIFAR-10. Finally, we prove a vanishing target theorem showing that the layer-wise learning signal decays with depth, providing a projection-based analogue of the vanishing gradients problem and clarifying one of the central barriers to scaling these methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern deep learning relies almost exclusively on gradient-based optimization, with gradients computed end-to-end via backpropagation. However, this paradigm imposes two structural assumptions: every component of the computational graph must admit a gradient (or a tractable subgradient), and parameter updates must propagate through the chain rule. To circumvent these limitations, many alternatives to backpropagation have been proposed, ranging from classical proposals such as target propagation \cite{lee2015difference} and the method of auxiliary coordinates \cite{carreira2014distributed}, to more recent schemes like forward-forward training \cite{hinton2022forward} and neuroevolution \cite{such2017deep}. Nevertheless, none of these paradigms have achieved widespread adoption, typically bottlenecked by their heuristic approximations and inability to computationally scale on modern hardware.

A promising alternative that has recently gained attention is to recast learning as a feasibility problem \cite{elser2019learning, bergmeister2025projection, elser2026learning}. Instead of minimizing a global loss function $\mathcal{L}$, this approach searches for some point $\tilde{\theta}$ in the intersection of multiple constraint sets. These sets are chosen in a way so that from a feasible point $\tilde{\theta}$, one can derive the parameters $\theta$ of the neural network such that the training data is perfectly interpolated. Conceptually, this paradigm shift takes the form
\begin{tcolorbox}[shift_card]
\[
\small
\minimize_{\theta \in \Theta}\, \mathcal{L}(f(x;\theta), y)
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\textnormal{find } \tilde{\theta} \in \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} C_i.
\]\end{tcolorbox}
A rich literature exists to tackle such problems, where projection-based methods are the most well-known. Examples of such methods include, but are not limited to, cyclic projections \cite{gubin1967method}, Dykstra's algorithm \cite{dykstra1983algorithm}, and averaged projections \cite{auslender_thesis}. In the two-set case, Douglas--Rachford splitting \cite{} has been studied extensively and applied across a wide range of problems \cite{bauschke2021douglasrachfordalgorithmsolvingpossibly, lindstrom2020surveyyearsdouglasrachford}. Convergence theory for these projection-based methods has been primarily developed in the convex setting \cite{}, while there have been recent advances in the nonconvex setting as well \cite{}.

While these projection-based algorithms are promising, they are not competitive with gradient-based methods, even for shallow networks. Moreover, they come at the cost of substantial memory overhead, which prohibits scaling at the hardware level. Concretely, there are currently three major limitations. First, the consensus step, explained in \cref{sec:nn_feas}, requires a duplication of the neural network parameters across all samples in a batch, yielding a severe memory consumption. Second, the graph-propagation scheme of \cite{bergmeister2025projection} requires splitting the computational graph into a bipartite graph for every batch, introducing a per-batch overhead absent from standard training pipelines. Third, the frameworks in \cite{elser2019learning,bergmeister2025projection} were developed largely in isolation from modern deep learning toolchains and therefore cannot benefit from the many software optimizations that underpin state-of-the-art libraries such as Torch \cite{paszke2019pytorchimperativestylehighperformance}. In particular, while PJAX \cite{bergmeister2025projection} is built on JAX \cite{}, it utilizes only the high-performance tensor computation library while all neural network training logic has been completely redefined within PJAX itself. These identified limitations naturally lead us to our research question.

%Recent work by Bergmeister \cite{bergmeister2025projection} and Elser \cite{elser2019learning} has shown that projection-based algorithms can train neural networks in practice, but at the cost of substantial memory overhead and with empirical performance that remains well below that of gradient-based training. A closer examination reveals three recurring bottlenecks. First, memory consumption is severe: the consensus step in the linear layer scales with $O(N_{\mathrm{in}}N_{\mathrm{out}}B)$ rendering training infeasible on commonly available hardware. Second, the graph-propagation scheme of~\cite{bergmeister2025projection} requires splitting the computational graph into a bipartite graph for every batch, introducing a per-batch overhead absent from standard training pipelines. Third, convergence is noticeably slower than Adam, even on small MLPs. Moreover, these frameworks were developed largely in isolation from modern deep learning toolchains and therefore cannot benefit from the many software optimizations that underpin state-of-the-art libraries such as Torch \cite{paszke2019pytorchimperativestylehighperformance}.\footnote{While PJAX is built on JAX, it utilizes the framework solely as a high-performance tensor computation library; all neural network training logic has been completely redefined within PJAX itself}.

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tcolorbox}[researchcard]
Can we design a projection-based training framework native to modern deep learning toolchains that eliminates severe memory bottlenecks, avoids computational overhead, and achieves performance competitive with gradient-based methods?
\end{tcolorbox}
\vspace{0.5em}

To address these issues, we developed $\mathcal{P}$Torch. The compatibility problem is resolved by building on PyTorch rather than JAX, and by performing graph traversal directly through the native \texttt{autograd}. As a result, the gap between gradient-based and projection-based optimization in our framework reduces, as closely as possible, to the gap between backpropagation and its projection-based counterpart isolating the optimization strategy from implementation artifacts. 

A further motivation for this line of research is the risk of paradigm lock-in: the overwhelming focus on gradient-based training may lead the community to overlook alternative learning frameworks that, given comparable research investment, could ultimately prove competitive or superior. Projection-based learning is still in its infancy, yet $\mathcal{P}$Torch already achieves competitive performance on shallow MLPs, suggesting that much of the gap is attributable to implementation artifacts rather than a fundamental limitation of the optimization strategy. 

Making projection-based learning a mainstream paradigm remains a long-term goal; as with gradient-based 
optimization, the right implementation tricks to train deep networks such as weight-initialization like He initialization \cite{delving} for gradients have yet to be fully worked out.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Target propagation.}
Target propagation is a  gradient-free learning paradigm where layer-wise targets are propagated backward through the network instead of gradients \cite{DTP}. While these methods attempt to generate these targets by approximating the inverse of the forward map, our projection-based approach instead treats layer-wise updates as local feasibility problems. Instead of relying on approximate inverses. Ptorch computes targets by mathematically projecting the current state onto the layer's constraint set to find the absolute smallest adjustment required to satisfy the network's architectural constraints. Despite these different mechanisms for generating local targets, both approaches share a fundamental limitation: the attenuation of the learning signal with depth. 

\paragraph{ADMM-based deep learning.}
The Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) offers a prominent alternative to the projection-based training of neural networks. Similar to projection-based methods, ADMM decouples the network into per-layer subproblems by introducing auxiliary variables and enforcing consistency constraints via a Lagrangian penalty. Wang et al.~\cite{10.1145/3292500.3330936} advanced this line of work with the dlADMM algorithm, which updates parameters in a backward-then-forward sweep to propagate information more efficiently. Crucially, their approach reduces the subproblem complexity from $O(n^3)$ to $O(n^2)$ using iterative quadratic approximations and backtracking, while also establishing rigorous global convergence guarantees under mild conditions on the loss and activation functions. Ultimately, the layer-wise decomposition in dlADMM conceptually aligns with $\mathcal{P}$Torch's target propagation: both methods bypass end-to-end differentiation by decomposing the global objective into isolated, layer-wise optimization steps propagated through the computational graph.

\paragraph{Gradient-free optimization frameworks.}
A variety of other gradient-free optimization methods have been proposed in the literature, including the Method of Auxiliary Coordinates \cite{carreira2014distributed}. By unfolding the network into layers, this approach replaces a complex nested optimization problem with local optimization problems at the level of individual layers. Distinct paradigms such as neuroevolution have also attracted significant attention and even inspired dedicated frameworks \cite{tang2022evojax}. Another notable alternative is the more recent Forward-Forward algorithm \cite{hinton2022forward}. However, a common thread across these approaches is that none has yet achieved widespread adoption in mainstream deep learning.

\section{Neural networks as a feasibility problem} \label{sec:nn_feas}

\subsection{The intuition behind the method}
In contrast to gradient-based optimization, projection-based learning reframes training as a feasibility problem rather than a purely loss-minimization problem.

Imagine the neural network as a sequence of interlocking constraints. Every layer has a strict rule it must satisfy (e.g., ``my output must exactly equal my weights multiplied by my input''). During a training step, we force the final output of the network to match the true target label. In doing so, we break the mathematical consistency of the layer immediately below it.

To restore consistency, we do not compute a gradient. Instead, we ask a simpler local question at each layer: \textit{``What is the smallest adjustment I can make to my weights, my incoming activations, and my output so that the constraint is perfectly satisfied again?''}

By repeatedly applying these local projections sequentially from the output back to the input of the network, we propagate a learning signal without ever requiring gradients.



\subsubsection{Single-layer update}
To bridge the gap between the mathematical formulation and the intuition, we explain the update for a single layer below.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\input{drawings/layer_example}
}
\caption{Update of the output linear layer: the projection acts as a mediator, taking the imposed output target $\bar{h}^{N}$ and distributing the necessary adjustments between the preceding hidden state $h^{N-1}$ and the weights $\theta^{N}$.}
\end{figure}

To see how this works mathematically, consider a single linear layer receiving a target from above. Let layer $N$ be a linear layer with weights $\theta^{N}$.

We define this layer's constraint as the set of all input weight output triples that satisfy the matrix multiplication relation:
$$A_{N} \;:=\; \bigl\{\bigl(h^{N-1},\, \theta^{N},\, h^{N}\bigr)
\,\bigm|\, h^{N} = \theta^{N} h^{N-1} \bigr\}.$$
When the network output is projected toward the true label, this layer receives a new target $\bar{h}^{N}$. The current weights and inputs no longer produce this target. To restore consistency, we project the current state onto the constraint set $A_{N}$. This yields updated values that satisfy the layer constraint, but the modified input to this layer then becomes inconsistent with the layer below.

A full walkthrough is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:walk-through}.


\subsection{The mathematical description}
Let $\{(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_B, y_B)\}$ be a dataset, and suppose we have a neural network with parameters $\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_N$. For ease of presentation, we assume the network has a chain structure, although the extension to more complex topologies is straightforward. In particular, the forward pass for a single sample $(x_b, y_b)$ can be written as
\begin{align*}
    h^{(0)}_b &= x_b \\
    h^{(i+1)}_b &= f_{i+1}(h^{(i)}_b, \theta^{(i+1)}), \quad i=0,\ldots,N-1
\end{align*}
for some functions $f_1, \ldots, f_N$. The output of the network is given by $h^N$. For example, if $\theta^i$ is a matrix and $\Sigma$ is an activation function,  then $f_i(h^{(i-1)}_b, \theta^{(i)}) = \theta^i h^{(i-1)}_b$ followed by $f_{i+1}(h^{(i)}_b, \theta^{(i+1)}) = \Sigma(h^{(i)}_b)$ is the standard building block of a multilayer perceptron. Note that $\theta^{(i+1)}$ is an ``empty'' variable in this case.

To model the network as a feasibility problem we can associate  each datapoint $(x_b, y_b)$ to the auxiliary variables $\Omega_i=\{h^{(0)}_b, \ldots, h^{(N)}_b, \theta^{(1)}_b, \ldots, \theta_b^{(N)}\}$. Then, there are three types of constraints that we consider:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A}rchitectural constraints: each variable must be a feasible output of its corresponding neural network operation.
    \item \textbf{B}atch (data) constraints: the inputs and outputs of the network are fixed to match the current training batch.
    \item \textbf{C}onsensus constraints: variables shared across multiple constraints must agree on a single consistent value.
\end{enumerate}


Our feasibility problem is then to find some $\omega = (\Omega_1, \ldots, \Omega_B)$
such that
\[
    \omega \in \left(\bigcap_{b=1}^B\bigcap_{i=1}^N A_{bi}\right) \bigcap \left(B_{\rm in} \cap B_{\rm out}\right) \bigcap \left(\bigcap_{i=1}^N C_i\right)
\]
where $A_{ij}$ denote the \textbf{A}rchitectural constraint sets, $B_{\rm in}$ and $B_{\rm out}$ denote the \textbf{B}atch constraint sets at the input and output respectively, and $C_j$ denote certain \textbf{C}onsensus constraint sets.

In particular, $A_{bi}$ states that $h_b^{(i)} = f_i(h_b^{(i-1)}, \theta_b^{(i)})$ for each $b=1,\ldots, B$, $i=1,\ldots, N$. Further, $B_{\rm in}$ requires $h_b^{(0)} = x_b$ while $B_{\rm out}$ requires $h_b^{(N)} = y_b$. Lastly, since we duplicated the neural network parameters $\theta^{(1)}, \ldots, \theta^{(N)}$ for each datapoint, they must all equal each other, i.e., $C_i$ ensures that $\theta_1^{(i)} = \cdots = \theta_b^{(i)}$ for each $i=1,\ldots, N$. If the problem is feasible, then an equivalent minimization problem is
\[
    \omega^\star \in \argmin_{\omega\in\Omega} \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sum_{b=1}^B \sum_{i=1}^N \dist^2(A_{bi}, \omega)   + \dist^2(B_{\rm in}, \omega) + \dist^2(B_{\rm out}, \omega) + \sum_{i=1}^N \dist^2(C_i, \omega)\right\}}_{\coloneqq F(\omega)}
\]
where $\dist^2(C, \omega) = \min_{y\in C} \|\omega - y\|^2$.
\label{sec:feasibility}
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tcolorbox}[shift_card]
To summarize, if we find an $\omega$ that resides in the intersection of these sets, then the parameters $\theta^{(1)}, \ldots, \theta^{(N)}$ (which are equal over all datapoints due to the consensus constraint) allow the neural network to perfectly interpolate the data.
\end{tcolorbox}
\vspace{0.5em}

\section{Cyclic projections}
Our basic algorithm for solving this feasibility problem is the cyclic projections method. Starting from initial variables $h^{(0)}_1, \ldots, h^{(N)}_1, \theta^{(1)}_1, \ldots, \theta^{(N)}_1, \ldots, h^{(0)}_B, \ldots, h^{(N)}_B, \theta^{(1)}_B, \ldots, \theta^{(N)}_B$  obtained from a single forward pass. The method reinterprets the backward pass as a sequence of local layer-wise  projections, yielding the following iterative procedure.
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Cyclic projections method.}\label{alg:cyclic}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $h^{(N)}_b \gets y_b$, $b=1,\ldots, B$  \Comment{enforce Batch constraints}
\For{$i=N-1$ \textbf{downto} $0$}\Comment{Traverses the computational graph backwards}
    \For{$b=1$ \textbf{upto} $B$}
        \State $(\bar h^{(i)}_b, \bar \theta^{(i+1)}_b, \bar h^{(i+1)}_b) \in P_{A_{b,i+1}}(h^{(i)}_b, \theta^{(i+1)}_b, h^{(i+1)}_b)$  \Comment{enforce Architectural Constraint}
        \State $(h^{(i)}_b, \theta^{(i+1)}_b, h^{(i+1}_b) \gets (\bar h^{(i)}_b, \bar \theta^{(i+1)}_b, \bar h^{(i+1)}_b)$
    \EndFor
    \State $\bar \theta^{(i+1)} \gets \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \theta_{b}^{(i+1)}$
    \State $\theta^{(i+1)}_b \gets \bar \theta^{(i+1)}$  \Comment{enforce Consensus Constraint}
\EndFor
\State $h^{(0)}_b \gets x_b, b=1,\ldots,B$ \Comment{enforce Batch constraints}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This interpretation is possible because we associate a projection operator with each functional node in the computational graph. As a result, the forward pass remains identical to its gradient-based counterpart, while the backward pass computes a projection rather than a gradient whenever a node is visited.

The basic algorithm can be improved in several ways. In particular, we refine its most sensitive components by developing an improved matrix multiplication projection for linear layers, incorporating Adam and Muon as nonlinear relaxation heuristics, and adopting the proximal loss step introduced in PJAX~\cite{bergmeister2025projection}.

\subsubsection{Connection to incremental gradient descent}
To motivate the constructions that follow, we make explicit the connection between cyclic projections and the incremental gradient method \cite{bertsekas1997nonlinear}. Recall from Section~\ref{sec:feasibility} that training reduces to the finite-sum problem $F(\omega)$
\[
    \min_{\omega} \; \frac{1}{2}\left\{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(\dist^{2}(A_{bi}, \omega) \;+\; \dist^{2}(B_{\mathrm{in}}, \omega) \;+\; \dist^{2}(B_{\mathrm{out}}, \omega) \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \dist^{2}(C_i, \omega)\bigr)\right\}
    \label{eq:finite_sum}
\]
which has the form $F(\omega) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} f_j(\omega)$, with each component $f_j(\omega) = \dist^{2}(C_j, \omega)$ a squared distance to a constraint set. Wherever the projection $P_{C_j}$ is single-valued, $f_j$ is differentiable with
\begin{equation}
    \nabla f_j(\omega) \;=\; \omega - P_{C_j}(\omega).
    \label{eq:dist_gradient}
\end{equation}

A subtlety is that the constraint sets induced by the network are non-convex, so $P_{C_j}(\omega)$ doesn't need not be unique. At points $\omega$ that are equidistant from two or more elements of $C_j$, the projection is ambiguous and~\eqref{eq:dist_gradient} is not well defined. This issue is benign for two reasons. First, the set of such points has Lebesgue measure zero, so~\eqref{eq:dist_gradient} holds almost everywhere by Rademacher's theorem. Second, on this exceptional set the residual $\omega - P_{C_j}(\omega)$ remains a valid subgradient under any choice of $P_{C_j}(\omega)$, so the update direction is well-defined in either case. The situation is identical to that of $\mathrm{ReLU}$ at the origin: non-differentiable at a single point, yet trained without difficulty using a subgradient selection.

With~\eqref{eq:dist_gradient} in hand, the cyclic projection update of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cyclic} is exactly an incremental gradient step on $F$. The plain projection step
\begin{equation}
    \bar \theta^{i+1} \;\gets\; P_{C_j}\!\bigl(\theta^{i+1}\bigr)
    \label{eq:plain_projection}
\end{equation}
can be rewritten as
\begin{equation} \
    \bar \theta^{(i+1)}\gets\; (1-\alpha)\,\theta^{i+1} \;+\; \alpha\, P_{C_j}\!\bigl(\theta^{i+1}\bigr)
    \qquad \alpha = 1,
    \label{eq:relaxed_projection}
\end{equation}
This is the relaxed projection with relaxation factor $\alpha$. Rearranging~\eqref{eq:relaxed_projection} and substituting~\eqref{eq:dist_gradient} gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar \theta^{i+1} \;\gets\; \theta^{i+1} \;-\; \alpha\bigl(\theta^{i+1} - P_{C_j}(\theta^{i+1})\bigr) \;=\; \theta^{i+1} \;-\; \alpha\,\nabla f_j\!\bigl(\theta^{i+1}\bigr),
    \label{eq:projection_as_gradient_step}
\end{equation}
which is precisely the incremental gradient update~\cite[Eq.~(2.78)]{bertsekas1997nonlinear}
\begin{equation}
    \omega^{k+1} \;=\; \omega^{k} \;-\; \alpha^{k}\,\nabla f_{j_k}(\omega^{k})
    \label{eq:incremental_gradient}
\end{equation}
applied to the finite-sum objective~\eqref{eq:finite_sum}, with step size $\alpha^k$ and index $j_k$ chosen by the cyclic order that traverses the computational graph. We are using the cyclic order index-selection rules listed in~\cite[\S2.4.1]{bertsekas1997nonlinear} correspond directly to natural variants of our scheme.
\subsection{Why the proximal loss step helps: a Moreau-envelope view}
\label{sec:moreau}
 
The rigid target-assignment step replaces the network's final prediction
$h^{N}$ with the true target $y$. The proximal-loss variant softens this
by replacing $h^{N}$ with a compromise between staying close to $h^{N}$
and reducing the loss $\mathcal{L}(\cdot,y)$. Concretely, given a
smoothing strength $\lambda>0$, we define
\begin{equation}
\bar h^{N} \;:=\; \arg\min_{u}\;
\underbrace{\mathcal{L}(u,y)}_{\text{loss at }u}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\bigl\|u-h^{N}\bigr\|^{2}}_{\text{stay near }h^{N}} ,
\label{eq:prox-def}
\end{equation}
and update $h^{N}\leftarrow\bar h^{N}$. We call $\bar h^{N}$ the
proximal target.
The minimum value attained in~\eqref{eq:prox-def} defines a new function
of $h^{N}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(h^{N},y)
\;:=\;
\min_{u}\Bigl\{\,\mathcal{L}(u,y)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl\|u-h^{N}\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr\}.
\label{eq:env-def}
\end{equation}
This is the Moreau envelope of $\mathcal{L}$ at strength $\lambda$.
It is a smoothed lower bound: $\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\le\mathcal{L}$ pointwise,
$\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\to\mathcal{L}$ as $\lambda\to\infty$, and
$\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}$ is differentiable in $h^{N}$ even when
$\mathcal{L}$ is not. A direct calculation gives the gradient in closed form:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\!h^{N}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(h^{N},y)
\;=\;
\lambda\,\bigl(h^{N}-\bar h^{N}\bigr).
\label{eq:moreau-grad}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:moreau-grad} is the key identity: the displacement
produced by the proximal step, scaled by $\lambda$, \emph{is} the gradient
of the smoothed loss. Consequently, the update $h^{N}\leftarrow\bar h^{N}$
is exactly one gradient-descent step of step size $1/\lambda$ on
$\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\cdot,y)$:
\[
h^{N}\;\leftarrow\;\bar h^{N}
\;=\;h^{N}-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\nabla_{\!h^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(h^{N},y).
\]
 Globally, the proximal-loss variant therefore optimizes the surrogate
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda}(\omega)
\;=\;
\Phi_{\mathrm{net}}(\omega)\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\!\bigl(h^{N}(\omega),\,y\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{\mathrm{net}}(\omega)$ collects all internal network terms.
The original (unsmoothed) target-assignment objective is recovered as
$\lambda\to 0^{+}$.

An important property of this is that the optimum is preserved. For convex $\mathcal{L}(\cdot,y)$, $\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}$ and $\mathcal{L}$ share the same set of minimizers for every $\lambda>0$~\cite{rockafellar1998variational}, so choosing $\lambda$ trades smoothness against surrogate tightness without moving the solution.

The empirical gain can be explained by the bounded backward signal: the residual that initiates the backward sweep is $\lambda(h^{N}-\bar h^{N})$, which for mean-squared-error loss equals $\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}(h^{N}-y)$, i.e.\ the rigid residual $h^{N}-y$ contracted by a factor in $(0,1)$. More generally, whenever $\mathcal{L}(\cdot,y)$ is convex the proximal residual is no larger than the rigid residual, and whenever $\mathcal{L}$ is itself Lipschitz (e.g.\ hinge, Huber) it is bounded uniformly in $h^{N}$ by the Lipschitz constant of $\mathcal{L}$. 
\Comment{Dit moet beter}

\begin{algorithm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $h^{(N)}_b \gets \operatorname{prox}_{\lambda \ell}\!\left(h^{(N)}_b,\, y_b\right), \quad b=1,\ldots,B$
\Comment{Proximal step $B_{\rm out}$; replaces  $h^{(N)}_b \gets y_b$}
\For{$i=N-1$ \textbf{downto} $0$}
\Comment{Traverses the computational graph}
    \State $\cdots$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsection{Efficient Linear Layer}
\subsubsection{Combined consensus and bilinear constraint in  $\ell_2$}

In standard projection-based training, the consensus projection for the linear layer is the primary memory bottleneck. A naive batched implementation requires building an $N_{\mathrm{in}} \times N_{\mathrm{out}} \times B $ tensor of rows (where $N_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the output dimension, $B$ is the batch size, and $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the input dimension) before averaging. This $O(N_{\mathrm{in}}N_{\mathrm{out}}B)$  memory footprint routinely causes out-of-memory errors on standard hardware.

To resolve this, we calculate the bilinear projection and the consensus linear projection simultaneously. By collapsing these steps, we eliminate the need to store a new set of weights for each individual sample within the batch loop, completely avoiding the  $O(N_{\mathrm{in}}N_{\mathrm{out}}B)$ memory blow-up.

Formally, this operation consists of two distinct projections: one onto the bilinear constraint set and one onto the consensus set. 

For a sample $b$ and output neuron $j$, the bilinear constraint $\theta^{i}[j,:]^{\top} h^{i}_{b} = h^{i+1}_{b}[j]$ is solved via Newton iteration to find a scalar $t_{jb}$ (see Appendix~\ref{app:bilinear-derivation}). Substituting the shared weight $\theta^{i}$ for the per-sample copy, the projected row becomes linear in $\theta^{i}[j,:]$ and $h^{i}_{b}$:
\begin{equation}
    \bar\theta^{i}_{b}[j,:] \;=\; \omega_{jb}\,\theta^{i}[j,:] \;+\; v_{jb}\, h^{i}_{b}{}^{\top}, \qquad \text{where} \quad \omega_{jb} := \frac{\alpha}{\alpha - t_{jb}^{2}}, \quad v_{jb} := \frac{t_{jb}}{\alpha - t_{jb}^{2}}.
\end{equation}

Second, the consensus projection forces all per-sample weights to agree by taking the batch average:
\begin{equation}
    \bar\theta^{i}[j,:] \;=\; \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \bar\theta^{i}_{b}[j,:].
\end{equation}

We can directly substitute the bilinear projection into the consensus average and factor out the shared parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \bar\theta^{i}[j,:] \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\!\left[\, \theta^{i}[j,:] \sum_{b=1}^{B} \omega_{jb} \;+\; \sum_{b=1}^{B} v_{jb}\, h^{i}_{b}{}^{\top} \right].
\end{equation}
By stacking the scalars $\omega_{jb}$ and $v_{jb}$ into matrices $\boldsymbol{\omega}, V \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}} \times B}$, and stacking the hidden states into $H \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N_{\mathrm{in}}}$, we can assemble the update across all output rows simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Theta} = \frac{1}{B} \left( \Theta \odot (\boldsymbol{\omega} \mathbf{1}_{B}) + V H \right).
\end{equation}

The second term is evaluated as a single $M \times B$ by $B \times K$ matrix multiplication. Because the calculations are collapsed, no 3D intermediate tensor is ever formed. Peak memory usage drops efficiently from $O(N_{\mathrm{in}}N_{\mathrm{out}}B)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{in}}B + N_{\mathrm{out}}B +N_{\mathrm{in}}N_{\mathrm{out}})$

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tcolorbox}[shift_card]
We will apply this combined consensus and operation projection universally. If a mathematically efficient join cannot be found, the consensus will be handled implicitly within the layer itself, thereby allowing us to remove the for-loop over the samples.
\end{tcolorbox}
\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Non-linear Relaxation}
\label{sec:relaxation}
A practical consequence of the incremental-gradient interpretation in the previous subsection is that any first-order optimizer designed for gradient updates such as SGD with momentum, Adam, or Muon can also be applied to the projection residual $\Delta\theta^{(i+1)}$. This preserves the empirical benefits of momentum and per-parameter scaling that drive modern gradient-based training, while leaving the underlying cyclic projection algorithm intact.

\paragraph{Relaxed projection.}
Plain cyclic projection overwrites the current iterate with its projected point. By contrast, relaxed variants, standard in the convex optimization literature, take a combination of the current iterate and the projection.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}[t]{0.56\textwidth}
        \vspace{-90pt}
        Let $\bar{\theta}^{(i+1)} := P_{C_j}(\theta^{(i+1)})$ denote the projected iterate and define the replace projection step       
        \begin{equation}
                    \theta^{(i+1)} \;\gets\; \theta^{(i+1)} \;+\; \eta\,\Delta\theta^{(i+1)}.
            \label{eq:eta_relaxed_projection}
        \end{equation}
        Here $\eta = 1$ recovers the exact projection onto the feasible set, $\eta = 2$ produces a full reflection through that boundary, and $\eta \in (0, 1)$ yields under-relaxed steps. Over-relaxed (reflected) projections can converge faster but are less stable, while under-relaxed projections trade speed for stability. The same trade-off familiar from gradient-based step-size selection.

\paragraph{Adaptive, per-parameter relaxation.}
There is no reason to restrict $\eta$ to a single scalar. Since~\eqref{eq:projection_as_gradient_step} shows that $\Delta\theta^{(i+1)}$ is a valid (sub)gradient of the finite-sum objective in~\eqref{eq:finite_sum}, the relaxation factor can vary across parameters and adapt over the course of training by feeding $\Delta\theta^{(i+1)}$ into a standard first-order optimizer in place of the gradient. Because $\mathcal{P}$Torch already routes projection residuals through PyTorch's \texttt{autograd}, no algorithmic modification is required: the residual is written to \texttt{.grad}, and the optimizer is invoked exactly as in a standard training loop.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \State $\cdots$
        \State $\bigl(h^{(i)}_b,\;\theta^{(i+1)}_b,\;h^{(i+1)}_b\bigr)
            \;\gets\;
            \operatorname{Optimizer}\!\left(
                \bar{h}^{(i)}_b - h^{(i)}_b,\;
                \bar{\theta}^{(i+1)}_b - \theta^{(i+1)}_b,\;
                \bar{h}^{(i+1)}_b - h^{(i+1)}_b
            \right)$
            \Comment{Relaxed projection via pseudo-gradient}
        \State $\cdots$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
In practice, we apply it to the weight residuals $\bar{\theta}^{(i+1)}_b - \theta^{(i+1)}_b$, leaving the hidden-state residuals as plain projections. In the view of incremental gradient descent this means we are applying standard  gradient-descent to the hidden states and accelerated gradient-descent to the weights. 

\subsection{Extension to Mini-Batches and the Full Algorithm}

The base algorithm is naturally formulated in the full-batch setting. To extend it to mini-batches, we introduce an inner loop that repeats the backward pass $K$ times for each batch. The hyperparameter $K$ determines how strictly the constraints induced by the current mini-batch are enforced before the algorithm proceeds to the next batch.

This creates a natural optimization trade-off. Larger values of $K$ allow the network to satisfy the current batch constraints more closely, but may lead to overfitting or cause training to stall. Conversely, smaller values of $K$ produce noisier, more stochastic dynamics, as the network switches more rapidly between the potentially conflicting constraints imposed by different mini-batches.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Mini-Batch $\mathcal{P}$Torch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $h^{(N)}_b \gets \operatorname{prox}_{\lambda \ell}\!\left(h^{(N)}_b,\, y_b\right), \quad b=1,\ldots,B$
\For{$k=1$ \textbf{to} $K$} \Comment{Repeat the backward sweep $K$ times}
    \For{$i=N-1$ \textbf{downto} $0$} \Comment{Traverse the computational graph backward}
        \State $(\bar h^{(i)}, \bar \theta^{(i+1)}, \bar h^{(i+1)}) \in P_{A_{i+1}}(h^{(i)}, \theta^{(i+1)}, h^{(i+1)})$ 
        \State $\bigl(h^{(i)},\;\theta^{(i+1)},\;h^{(i+1)}\bigr) \gets \operatorname{Optimizer}\!\left( \bar{h}^{(i)} - h^{(i)},\; \bar{\theta}^{(i+1)} - \theta^{(i+1)},\; \bar{h}^{(i+1)} - h^{(i+1)} \right)$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State $h^{(0)}_b \gets x_b$ for $b=1,\ldots,B$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Note that the explicit \texttt{for} loop over individual samples within a batch has been omitted. We assume that each projection operator has an integrated consensus calculation and processes the entire mini-batch in parallel, analogous to the behavior of standard deep learning frameworks.

\section{Theoretical Analysis: Vanishing Target Signals in Deep Networks}
A fundamental limitation of sequential target propagation in deep architectures is the attenuation of the learning signal with depth. We formalize this phenomenon below.

At training step $n$, define the \emph{target signal magnitude} at hidden layer $i$ by
\[
\delta^{i} \;:=\; \bigl\|\bar{h}^{i} - h^{i}\bigr\|_2.
\]

\begin{assumption}[Local Non-Expansiveness]
\label{ass:non-expansive}
Let the constraint set associated with layer $i+1$ be

\[
A_{i+1} \;:=\; \{(h^{(i)}, \theta^{(i+1)}, h^{(i+1)}) \mid h^{(i+1)} = f_{i+1}(h^{(i)}, \theta^{(i+1)})\}.
\]

Although $A_{i+1}$ is generally non-convex due to nonlinearities and bilinear weight--activation interactions, we assume that, within a sufficiently small neighborhood of the current iterates, the projection $P_{A_{i+1}}$ behaves non-expansively. This assumption is empirically supported in Appendix~\ref{sec:Non-Expansiveness}.
\end{assumption}

Under Assumption~\ref{ass:non-expansive}, the target signal magnitude $\delta^{(i), n}$ is monotonically non-increasing as targets are propagated backward from the output layer $N$:
\[
\delta^{i} \;\leq\; \delta^{i+1} \quad \text{for all } 0 \leq i \leq N-1.
\]
Whenever learning occurs, that is, whenever $\|\Delta \theta^{(i+1)}\| > 0$, the inequality is strict.

We proceed by induction moving backward from the output layer $N$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Base case.}
At the output layer $N$, the proximal update for an arbitrary loss function $\mathcal{L}$ gives

$$\bar{h}^{(N), n} \;\in\; \underset{h}{\mathrm{arg\,min}} \left( \mathcal{L}\bigl(h, y_{\mathrm{target}}^{n}\bigr) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \bigl\|h - h^{N}\bigr\|_2^2 \right).$$

Assuming $\mathcal{L}$ is proper and lower semi-continuous, this minimum is attained and bounded, so $\delta^{N} = \|\bar{h}^{N} - h^{N}\|_2 < \infty$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Inductive step.}
Suppose $\delta^{i+1}$ is finite. Consider the two points in the joint space $(h^{i}, \theta^{i+1}, h^{i+1})$:
\begin{align*}
A &= \bigl(h^{i},\; \theta^{i+1},\; \bar{h}^{i+1}\bigr), \\
B &= \bigl(h^{i},\; \theta^{i+1},\; h^{i+1}\bigr).
\end{align*}
Since the forward pass satisfies the constraint exactly, $B \in A_{i+1}$ and hence $P_{A_{i+1}}(B) = B$. By Assumption~\ref{ass:non-expansive}:

$$\|P_{A_{i+1}}(A) - P_{A_{i+1}}(B)\|_2^2 \;\leq\; \|A - B\|_2^2.$$

Expanding both sides and using the fact that $A$ and $B$ agree in their first two components:

$$\|\bar{h}^{(i), n} - h^{(i), n}\|_2^2 + \|\Delta \theta^{(i+1)}\|_2^2 + \| h^{(i+1), n+1} - h^{(i+1), n}\|_2^2
\;\leq\;
\|\bar{h}^{(i+1), n} - h^{(i+1), n}\|_2^2,$$

which gives $\bigl(\delta^{(i), n}\bigr)^2 \leq \bigl(\delta^{(i+1), n}\bigr)^2 - \bigl(\|\Delta \theta^{(i+1)}\|_2^2 + \|\Delta h^{(i+1)}\|_2^2\bigr) \leq \bigl(\delta^{(i+1), n}\bigr)^2$, and therefore $\delta^{(i), n} \leq \delta^{(i+1), n}$.

By induction this generalizes to $\delta^{(i), n} \leq \delta^{(i+1), n}$ for all $i$. When $\|\Delta \theta^{(i+1)}\|_2^2 > 0$ at every layer (i.e., the network is actively learning), each term is strictly smaller than the previous one.

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{tcolorbox}[shift_card]
Depth Barrier: In a network that is actively learning, cyclic projections result in a diminishing learning signal to early layers. For networks of sufficient depth, the target difference at the earliest layers reaches machine precision, rendering parameter updates in those layers negligible.
\end{tcolorbox}
\vspace{0.5em}

While no definitive solution to this problem has been found, several workarounds have been explored, including skip connections as in PJAX~\cite{bergmeister2025projection} and applying Muon's \cite{jordan2024muon} Newton--Schulz orthogonalization, without rescaling, to the hidden states while using a separate optimizer for the weights. These heuristics substantially improve performance in deeper networks, largely by stabilizing the target differences across layers. However, performance remains significantly weaker than that of traditional gradient-based learning, making this framework impractical for training deep networks at larger scale. For this reason, we do not include larger-scale experiments.

\section{A Walk Through Cyclic Projections}
\label{app:walk-through}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------
 
This section unrolls Algorithm~\ref{alg:cyclic-projections} for one batch on a feedforward
network, making explicit how the abstract projection $P_{A_{b,i+1}}$ instantiates for the
two canonical building blocks of an MLP (a linear sublayer and an elementwise activation),
and how the proximal output step of Section~\ref{sec:moreau} and the first-order relaxation
of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear-relaxation} slot into the per-layer updates.
 
\subsection{Forward and Backward Semantics}
 
Consider a feedforward network with $N$ layers as in Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}, with
computational graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. Each node $v \in \mathcal{V}$
corresponds to a layer function $f_i$ acting on the previous hidden state and (possibly empty)
parameters,
\[
h^{(i)} \;=\; f_i\bigl(h^{(i-1)}, \theta^{(i)}\bigr), \qquad i = 1, \dots, N,
\]
with $h^{(0)} = x$ and network output $h^{(N)}$. To exploit the native \texttt{autograd}
engine, $\mathcal{P}$Torch redefines the semantics of the forward and backward passes for each node.
 
\begin{definition}[Training-Step Iterates]
\label{def:iterates}
At training step $n$, the \emph{forward pass} produces the current hidden-state and parameter
iterates $\bigl\{h^{(i),n}\bigr\}_{i=0}^{N}$ and $\bigl\{\theta^{(i),n}\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N}$.
The \emph{backward pass} produces intermediate targets $\bigl\{\bar{h}^{(i),n},\,
\bar{\theta}^{(i),n}\bigr\}$ and the next iterates $\bigl\{h^{(i),n+1},\,
\theta^{(i),n+1}\bigr\}$.
\end{definition}
 
For ease of presentation we suppress the batch index $b$ throughout this section; the
extension is the per-sample procedure of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cyclic-projections}, with the
consensus step $C_i$ averaging $\bar{\theta}^{(i),n}_b$ across the batch as in
lines~7--8 of that algorithm.
 
\subsection{Loss Projection and Target Initialization}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\label{fig:init_back_prop}

Let $y^{n}$ denote the data target for batch $n$. The backward pass is initialized by the
proximal step on the output layer.
 
\begin{definition}[Output Target]
\label{def:output-target}
The target for the network output $\bar{h}^{(N),n}$ is the proximal step
\begin{equation}
\bar{h}^{(N),n} \;=\; \mathrm{prox}_{\mathcal{L}/\lambda}\!\bigl(h^{(N),n},\, y^{n}\bigr)
\;\in\; \underset{u}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}
\left( \mathcal{L}\bigl(u, y^{n}\bigr) + \frac{\lambda}{2}
       \bigl\|u - h^{(N),n}\bigr\|_2^2 \right).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
 
This $\bar{h}^{(N),n}$ replaces the hard projection onto the data set
$B_{\mathrm{out}} = \{h^{(N)} = y\}$ used in the base cyclic algorithm (line~1 of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cyclic-projections}); it is then propagated to the preceding layer as the
target $\bar{h}^{(N),n}$ entering the projection $P_{A_{N}}$.
 
A proximal operator is used here rather than a hard projection onto $B_{\mathrm{out}}$ to
accommodate loss functions for which the solution set is difficult to characterize
explicitly. For instance, it is not immediately clear how to define the feasible set
associated with binary cross-entropy, let alone how to project onto it directly. By
contrast, hard-margin constraint sets have a natural geometric interpretation and admit
straightforward projections. The proximal formulation thus provides a unified treatment that
remains tractable even when direct projection is ill-defined; as shown in
Section~\ref{sec:moreau}, it also corresponds to a gradient step on the Moreau envelope
$e_{1/\lambda}\mathcal{L}$.
 
\subsection{Layer-wise Propagation}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\input{drawings/next_step_backprop}
}
\caption{Update of the output linear layer: projection onto the architectural constraint
$A_{N}$ couples the output target $\bar{h}^{(N),n}$ to the deepest hidden state
$h^{(N-1),n}$ and the weights $\theta^{(N),n}$.}
\label{fig:next_back_prop}
\end{figure}
 
Assume layer $N$ is linear with weights $\theta^{(N)}$, so
$f_N\bigl(h^{(N-1)},\theta^{(N)}\bigr) = \theta^{(N)} h^{(N-1)}$. The corresponding
architectural constraint set is
\[
A_{N} \;:=\; \bigl\{\bigl(h^{(N-1)},\, \theta^{(N)},\, h^{(N)}\bigr)
\,\bigm|\, h^{(N)} = \theta^{(N)} h^{(N-1)} \bigr\}.
\]
 
\begin{proposition}[Linear-Layer Update]
\label{prop:linear-layer}
Given the current iterate $\bigl(h^{(N-1),n},\, \theta^{(N),n},\, h^{(N),n}\bigr)$ and the
output target $\bar{h}^{(N),n}$ from Definition~\ref{def:output-target}, the projection
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\bar{h}^{(N-1),n},\; \bar{\theta}^{(N),n},\; \bar{h}^{(N),n}\bigr)
\;\in\; P_{A_{N}}\!\bigl(h^{(N-1),n},\; \theta^{(N),n},\; \bar{h}^{(N),n}\bigr)
\end{equation}
simultaneously yields the target hidden state $\bar{h}^{(N-1),n}$ and the projected weights
$\bar{\theta}^{(N),n}$. Following Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear-relaxation}, the next-step
parameter iterate is obtained by feeding the projection residual into the chosen first-order
optimizer:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(N),n+1}
\;=\; \mathrm{Optimizer}\!\bigl(\theta^{(N),n},\; \eta,\;
       \theta^{(N),n} - \bar{\theta}^{(N),n}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with relaxation/step-size $\eta$. For plain STP this reduces to the exact projection
$\theta^{(N),n+1} = \bar{\theta}^{(N),n}$. The target $\bar{h}^{(N-1),n}$ then becomes the
output target entering the projection $P_{A_{N-1}}$ of the preceding (activation) layer.
\end{proposition}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\resizebox{0.78\textwidth}{!}{%
\input{drawings/next_update_backprop}
}
\caption{Projection through the activation constraint $A_{N-1}$, which carries no
parameters.}
\label{fig:next_back_prop_mid}
\end{figure}
 
Now consider the preceding activation layer $f_{N-1} = \sigma$, possibly non-differentiable.
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}, $\theta^{(N-1)}$ is empty for activation layers,
so the architectural constraint reduces to a relation between two hidden states:
\[
A_{N-1} \;:=\; \bigl\{\bigl(h^{(N-2)},\, h^{(N-1)}\bigr) \,\bigm|\,
h^{(N-1)} = \sigma\bigl(h^{(N-2)}\bigr) \bigr\}.
\]
 
\begin{proposition}[Activation-Layer Update]
\label{prop:activation-layer}
Given the target $\bar{h}^{(N-1),n}$ inherited from
Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-layer}, the projection
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\bar{h}^{(N-2),n},\; \bar{h}^{(N-1),n}\bigr)
\;=\; P_{A_{N-1}}\!\bigl(h^{(N-2),n},\; \bar{h}^{(N-1),n}\bigr)
\end{equation}
yields the target $\bar{h}^{(N-2),n}$ for the preceding layer.
\end{proposition}
 
This sequential procedure repeats for $i = N-2, N-3, \dots, 1$, alternating
Propositions~\ref{prop:linear-layer} and \ref{prop:activation-layer} as the layer types
dictate, until the input data constraint $B_{\mathrm{in}}: h^{(0)} = x$ closes the sweep
(line~10 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cyclic-projections}).


\subsection{Forward and Backward Semantics}

Consider a neural network structured as a computational chain $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. To exploit the native \texttt{autograd} engine, $\mathcal{P}$Torch redefines the semantics of the forward and backward passes for each node $v \in \mathcal{V}$.

\begin{definition}[Training Step Variables]
During training step $n$, for each node $v$ we denote its input by $h_v$ and its output by $q_v$, so that the output of one node feeds the input of the next: $h_{v} = q_{v-1}$. The \emph{forward pass} computes the current iterates $(h^{n}, q^{n})$. The backward pass computes intermediate targets $(\bar{h}^{n}, \bar{q}^{n})$ and the subsequent iterates $(h^{n+1}, q^{n+1})$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Loss Projection and Target Initialization}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\input{drawings/init_state_backprop}
}
\caption{First projection of the backward pass: coupling the network output to the loss constraint.}
\label{fig:init_back_prop}
\end{figure}

Let $y^{n}$ denote the data target for batch $n$. The backward pass is initialized by projecting the output onto the loss-minimizing constraint.

\begin{definition}[Output Target]
\label{def:output-target}
The loss layer input target $\bar{h}_{L+1}^n$ is defined via the proximal operation
\begin{equation}
\bar{h}_{L+1}^n \;\in\; \underset{h}{\mathrm{arg\,min}} \left( \mathcal{L}\bigl(h, y_{\mathrm{target}}^{n}\bigr) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \bigl\|h - h_{L+1}^{n}\bigr\|_2^2 \right).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The target for the output layer is set to $q_{L} = \bar{h}_{L+1}^n$, which is then passed directly as the target to the preceding layer.
\\
A proximal operator is used here rather than a projection to accommodate loss functions for which the solution set is difficult to characterize explicitly. For instance, it is not immediately clear how to define the feasible set associated with binary cross-entropy, let alone how to project onto it directly. By contrast, hard-margin constraint sets have a natural geometric interpretation and admit straightforward projections. The proximal formulation thus provides a unified treatment that remains tractable even when direct projection is ill-defined.

\subsection{Layer-wise Propagation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\input{drawings/next_step_backprop}
}
\caption{Update of the output weights: coupling the output loss with the deepest hidden layer.}

\end{figure}

The target output $\bar{q}_L^{n} ==  \bar{h}_{L+1}^n$ propagates to the preceding layer. The target input $\bar{h}_{L}^{n}$ and updated weights $W_L^{n+1}$ are obtained by projecting onto the feasibility set:

\begin{proposition}[Linear Layer Update]
\label{prop:linear-layer}
Define the bilinear feasibility set $C_{\mathrm{lin}} := \{(h, W, q) \mid q = Wh\}$. The projection
\begin{align}
\bigl(\bar{h}_{L}^{n},\; \bar{W}_L^{n},\; q_L^{n+1}\bigr)
  &\;\in\; P_{C_{\mathrm{lin}}}\!\bigl(h_{L}^{n},\; W_L^{n},\; \bar{q}_L^n\bigr)
\end{align}
simultaneously yields the subsequent output iterate $q_L^{n+1}$ and the target input $\bar{h}_{L}^{n}$ for the layer. Since the input of layer $L$ is the output of layer $L{-}1$, we have $\bar{q}_{L-1}^{n} = \bar{h}_{L}^{n}$. The updated weights are obtained by applying the optimizer to the pseudo-gradient direction, $W_L^{n+1} = \mathrm{Opt}\!\bigl(W_L^{n},\,\eta,\,W_L^{n}-\bar{W}_L^{n}\bigr)$ with step size $\eta$. For regular STP, this is the exact projection update $W_L^{n+1} = \bar{W}_L^{n}$.

\end{proposition}

}


\caption{Projection through the nonlinear activation constraint.}

\begin{proposition}[Activation Layer Update]
\label{prop:activation-layer}
Let $\sigma$ be a (possibly non-differentiable) activation function and define the activation feasibility set $C_{\sigma} := \{(h, q) \mid q = \sigma(h)\}$. Given the target $\bar{q}_{L-1}^{n}$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-layer}, the projection
\begin{align}
\bigl(\bar{h}_{L-1}^{n},\; q_{L-1}^{n+1}\bigr)
&\;=\; P_{C_{\sigma}}\!\bigl(h_{L-1}^{n},\; \bar{q}_{L-1}^{n}\bigr)
\end{align}
yields the subsequent output iterate $q_{L-1}^{n+1}$ and the target input $\bar{h}_{L-1}^{n}$, which becomes the target output $\bar{q}_{L-2}^{n}$ for the preceding layer.
\end{proposition}

This sequential projection procedure repeats, propagating targets backward through the computational graph $\mathcal{G}$ until the root is reached.

\section{Tuning projection based training}
To make projection-based neural network training algorithms more accessible for future researchers, this section is dedicated to discussing implementation and tuning details to ensure successful experiments.
\subsubsection{Tuning order}

Selecting the right optimizer is the most critical decision in this framework. In nearly all scenarios, the Muon projection optimizer yields the best results and is the recommended default. If you require faster initial convergence, SGD is a better alternative. Adams performance has a lot of potential but is very unstable and thus if you use it prepare to fine tune the learning rate a bit before its stable.

When utilizing SGD or Adadelta, higher learning rates even values such as 10, tend to perform better. A reliable tuning strategy is to start at 1 and adjust upward or downward based on training stability.

Regarding batch sizes: when using the $\ell_2$ projection, larger batch sizes such as 256 or 512 generally improve results. Scaling beyond this point has not shown further benefits and may degrade performance. Conversely, if the $\ell_{inf}$ linear layer is used, a smaller batch size of 32 has proven more effective in testing.

When designing architectures for this framework, prioritize shallow networks, as they outperform deeper architectures and maintain better stability. Widths of up to 1024 have been tested and exhibit very rapid convergence. Adding Batch Normalization or similar normalization layers significantly degrades performance and should currently be avoided.

Furthermore, take special care to ensure all layers influencing the gradient are implemented via  \emph{P}Torch modules. Using PyTorch layers, for example their linear layer will lead to autograd getting confused as either it tries to process targets as gradients or vice versa. To implement custom architectures like residual connections, refer to the implementations in the \texttt{overides.py} or residual networks benchmark file.

\subsubsection{Projection parameters}

The behavior of the projection can be fine-tuned by adjusting the $\alpha$ and g parameters, which determine whether the weights or activations absorb the bulk of the updates. Setting g to $\inf$ results in the output values not being changed, setting $\alpha$ to it will result in the weights not being changed. While the framework is robust and generally insensitive to these changes, precise adjustment can yield marginal gains when maximizing accuracy is sought. using an alpha and g in the range of [0.01-500] are logical values. \section{Hybrid architectures}
To evaluate the framework's interoperability with standard gradient-based modules, we explore a hybrid architectural design. Motivated by the current absence of a native projection-based embedding layer in \texttt{$\mathcal{P}$Torch}, we tackle the SST-2 text classification benchmark by combining optimization paradigms. Specifically, we construct a model utilizing a standard gradient-optimized embedding layer feeding into a projection-optimized Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) within \texttt{$\mathcal{P}$Torch}. For comparison, we evaluate a fully gradient-based equivalent in \texttt{torch} and an analogous architecture in \texttt{pjax}. This hybrid approach highlights the flexibility of \texttt{$\mathcal{P}$Torch}, demonstrating that researchers can seamlessly bridge projection-based and gradient-based training within a single computational graph.

\subsection{Layers}

\begin{tcolorbox}[projectioncard, title=Linear Layer]
\textbf{Constraint}
\[
C_{\mathrm{dot}} = \{(h, \theta, h^+) \mid \theta^\top h = h^+\}
\]

\textbf{Projection}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\overline{h} &= \frac{h + t\,\theta}{1-t^2}, \\
\overline{\theta} &= \frac{\theta + t\,h}{1-t^2}, \\
\overline{h}^+ &= h^+ - \frac{t}{\gamma^2}
\end{aligned}
\]
\textbf{Derivation Source}
\cite{bauschke_bilinear, elser2019learning, bergmeister2025projection}
\textit{The scalar $t$ is computed via Newton's method.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\paragraph{deriving the weighted projection}
As section \ref{sec:matmul} builds up on a weighted version of this derivation, the full derivation is placed below for the linear layer constraint in l2 using lagrangian multipliers as in \cite{elser2019learning}.
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constraint-dot}
  C_{\mathrm{dot}} := \{(h, \theta, h^+)\in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R} \mid \theta^\top h = h^+\}.
\end{equation}
So we are searching for the closest point in the feasibility set under a weighted distance norm:
\[
P_{C_{\mathrm{dot}}}^{\alpha, \gamma}(h, \theta, h^+) \in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(\overline{h}, \overline{\theta}, \overline{h}^+) \in C_{\mathrm{dot}}} \left( \lVert \overline{h}-h \rVert^2 + \alpha^2\lVert \overline{\theta}-\theta \rVert^2 + \gamma^2(\overline{h}^+ - h^+)^2 \right).
\]

Given a current iterate $(h, \theta, h^+)$ and scaling parameters $\alpha, \gamma>0$, the projection is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:proj-bilinear-layer}
  \begin{aligned}
    \min_{\overline{h}^+\in\mathbb{R},\,\overline{h},\overline{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^n}\; &\ \lVert \overline{h}-h\rVert^2 + \alpha^2\lVert \overline{\theta}-\theta\rVert^2 + \gamma^2\,(\overline{h}^+-h^+)^2\\
    \text{s.t. } &\ \overline{h}^+ = \overline{\theta}^\top \overline{h}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Introduce a Lagrangian multiplier $\lambda$ and define
\[
  \mathcal{L} = \lVert \overline{h}-h\rVert^2 + \alpha^2\lVert \overline{\theta}-\theta\rVert^2 + \gamma^2(\overline{h}^+-h^+)^2 + \lambda\,(\overline{h}^+ - \overline{\theta}^\top \overline{h}).
\]
Setting $\nabla \mathcal{L} = 0$ and using $t:=\lambda/2$ yields
\[
  \overline{h} = h + t\,\overline{\theta},\qquad \overline{\theta} = \theta + \frac{t}{\alpha^2}\,\overline{h},\qquad \overline{h}^+ = h^+ - \frac{t}{\gamma^2}.
\]
Solving the coupled system gives
\[
  \overline{h} = \frac{\alpha^2}{\alpha^2-t^2}(h + t\,\theta),\qquad \overline{\theta} = \frac{\alpha^2\theta }{\alpha^2-t^2} +  \frac{t\,h}{\alpha^2-t^2} .
\]
Substituting into the constraint $\overline{h}^+=\overline{\theta}^\top \overline{h}$ leads to a scalar equation $f(t)=0$:
\[
  h^+ - \frac{t}{\gamma^2} 
  = \frac{(\alpha^2\theta+t h)^\top \alpha^2(h+t \theta)}{(\alpha^2-t^2)^2} 
  = \alpha^2 \frac{p(\alpha^2+t^2)+q t}{(\alpha^2-t^2)^2},
\]
where $p:=\theta^\top h$ and $q:=\lVert h\rVert^2 + \alpha^2\lVert \theta\rVert^2$.
The root can be found efficiently with Newton's method, after which $\overline{h}, \overline{\theta}, \overline{h}^+$ are reconstructed from the expressions above.
\paragraph{$\ell_\infty$ derivation}
\label{sec:bilinear-inf}
\label{sec:linf}
The optimization geometry of the projection step can be modified to achieve
different training properties. We replace the standard Euclidean projection with
the $\ell_\infty$ one.
To show the derivation of this we will be using a simplified notation: $\theta:=\theta[j,:]\quad h^+:=h[j]^{+}$.

Given the current state $(h, \theta, h^{+}) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \times
\mathbb{R}$ and a scaling parameter $\gamma > 0$, the $\ell_\infty$ bilinear
projection problem is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bilinf-proj-bilinear-layer}
\min_{\epsilon}\; \epsilon \quad 
\text{s.t.} \quad \exists\, \bar h,\bar \theta, \bar h^{+} \colon\; \quad
\begin{aligned}
    \|\bar h-h\|_\infty &\leq \epsilon \\
    \|\bar \theta- \theta\|_\infty &\leq \epsilon \\
    \gamma^2 |\bar h^{+}- h^{+}| \, \, &\leq \epsilon \\
     \bar{\theta}^\top \bar h  &= \bar h^+
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The key is finding the smallest feasible box radius $\epsilon^* \geq 0$ such
that all constraints are simultaneously satisfiable.

Let $\Delta h = \bar h - h$ and $\Delta \theta = \bar \theta - \theta$. The
constraints read
\[
  |\Delta h_k| \leq \epsilon,\quad |\Delta \theta_{k}| \leq \epsilon
  \quad (k=1,\dots,n),
  \qquad |\bar h^{+} - h^{+}| \leq \frac{\epsilon}{\gamma^2}.
\]
The total dot-product change decomposes coordinate-wise. Let $D = \theta^\top h$ and $\bar{D} = \bar{\theta}^\top \bar{h}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bilinf-DeltaP}
\begin{aligned}
 \bar{D} &= (\theta + \Delta \theta)^\top (h + \Delta h) \\
  \Delta D_k 
    \;&=\; (h_k+\Delta h_k)(\theta_{k}+\Delta \theta_{k}) - h_k \theta_{k} \\
    \;&=\; h_k\,\Delta \theta_{k} + \theta_{k}\,\Delta h_k + \Delta h_k\,\Delta \theta_{k}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For any coordinate $k$, the extrema of $\Delta D_k$ over
the box $[-\epsilon,\epsilon]^2$ are attained at the four corners
$(\pm\epsilon, \pm\epsilon)$.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\hline
$\Delta h_k$ & $\Delta \theta_{k}$ & $\Delta D_k$ \\
\hline
$+\epsilon$ & $+\epsilon$ & $\phantom{-}\epsilon(h_k+\theta_{k})+\epsilon^2$ \\
$-\epsilon$ & $-\epsilon$ & $-\epsilon(h_k+\theta_{k})+\epsilon^2$ \\
\hline
$+\epsilon$ & $-\epsilon$ & $\phantom{-}\epsilon(\theta_{k}-h_k)-\epsilon^2$ \\
$-\epsilon$ & $+\epsilon$ & $\phantom{-}\epsilon(h_k-\theta_{k})-\epsilon^2$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The maximum upward contribution of coordinate $k$ to the dot product is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bilinf-Mk}
  M_k(\epsilon)
    \;=\; \max\!\Bigl(
      \epsilon|h_k+\theta_{k}|+\epsilon^2,\;
      \epsilon|h_k-\theta_{k}|-\epsilon^2
    \Bigr).
\end{equation}

In the case $D=\theta^\top h < h^{+}$, the smallest feasible radius $\epsilon^*$ is
the unique positive root of
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bilinf-root}
\begin{aligned}
    D+\sum_{k=1}^{n} M_k(\epsilon)
      &= h^{+}  - \frac{\epsilon}{\gamma^2}, \\
    F(\epsilon)
      &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} M_k(\epsilon) + \frac{\epsilon}{\gamma^2} - (h^{+} - D)
      \;=\; 0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$F$ is continuous, strictly monotone increasing, and piecewise quadratic
in $\epsilon$.

Each coordinate contributes at most one kink to $F$, occurring at
\[
  \epsilon_k^{\mathrm{kink}}
    \;=\; \max\!\left\{0,\;
      \frac{|h_k-\theta_{k}|-|h_k+\theta_{k}|}{2}
    \right\}.
\]
Sorting these kink points requires $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$ time, with root
evaluation linear in $n$ on each interval.

\paragraph{$\mathcal{O}(n)$ Solve via Branch Masking}
Define $a_k := |h_k+\theta_{k}|$ and $b_k := |h_k-\theta_{k}|$.
Coordinate $k$ contributes via whichever of two geometrically distinct regimes yields the larger reach:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Same-sign branch} ($a_k\epsilon + \epsilon^2$): the box pushes
        $h_k$ and $\theta_{k}$ in the \emph{same} direction, expanding their sum.
        This branch dominates when $\epsilon$ is large relative to the gap
        $b_k - a_k$.
  \item \textbf{Opp-sign branch} ($b_k\epsilon - \epsilon^2$): the box pushes
        $h_k$ and $\theta_{k}$ in \emph{opposite} directions, collapsing their
        difference. This branch dominates when $\epsilon$ is small.
\end{itemize}
The crossover between branches occurs at the unique kink
$\epsilon_k^{\mathrm{kink}} = \max\{0, (b_k - a_k)/2\}$.
For $\epsilon$ above this threshold the same-sign branch wins; below it the
opp-sign branch wins. This is encoded by the binary mask
\[
  m_k(\epsilon) \;:=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left[2\epsilon \geq b_k - a_k\right],
\]
so that the per-coordinate reach takes the \emph{unified} branch-free form
\begin{equation}
  M_k(\epsilon)
    \;=\; m_k(\epsilon)\bigl(a_k\epsilon+\epsilon^2\bigr)
      + \bigl(1-m_k(\epsilon)\bigr)\bigl(b_k\epsilon-\epsilon^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Since $m_k(\epsilon)$ is a step function that flips \emph{at most once} as
$\epsilon$ increases, both $F(\epsilon)$ and its derivative
\begin{equation}
  F'(\epsilon)
    \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl[
        m_k(\epsilon)\bigl(a_k + 2\epsilon\bigr)
      + \bigl(1-m_k(\epsilon)\bigr)\bigl(b_k - 2\epsilon\bigr)
    \Bigr] + \frac{1}{\gamma^2}
\end{equation}
are computable in a single $\mathcal{O}(n)$ pass by evaluating both branch
expressions and selecting via $m_k$, \emph{without sorting or branching in
control flow}. Because $F$ is continuous, strictly increasing, and piecewise
quadratic with a uniformly positive derivative (guaranteed by the
$1/\gamma^2$ term), Newton's method is well-conditioned. The safeguarded
iteration
\[
  \epsilon_{t+1}
    \;=\; \max\!\left(\ell_t,\min\!\left(r_t, \epsilon_t - \frac{F(\epsilon_t)}{F'(\epsilon_t)}\right)\right)
\]
(with $[\ell_t, r_t]$ a bisection bracket maintained as a fallback) converges
in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ iterations in practice: the piecewise-quadratic structure means each Newton step either lands in the correct quadratic segment immediately, or crosses at most one kink before the next iterate is already in the final segment. The full solve is therefore $\mathcal{O}(n)$.

\begin{aligned}
p_1 &= (\min(x_0,0),\, 0) \\
p_2 &= (\max(0,\bar{x}),\, \max(0,\bar{x})), \quad 
\bar{x} = \frac{x_0 + y_0}{2}
\end{aligned}
\]

Select $p \in \{p_1,p_2\}$ minimizing Euclidean distance.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[projectioncard, title=Margin Loss]
\textbf{Constraint}
\[
S_{\mathrm{margin}} = \{(x,y) \mid x \leq 0,\; y \geq 0\}
\]

\textbf{Projection}
\[
P_{C_{y,m}}(x_0) =
\begin{cases}
\min(x_0,0), & y \leq 0 \\
\max(x_0,m), & y > 0
\end{cases}
\]
\end{tcolorbox}
\textbf{Derivation Source} \cite{elser2019learning}\cite{bergmeister2025projection}

\textbf{Objective}
\[
\mathrm{CE}(x,y) = \log \sum_{j=1}^d e^{x_j} - \langle x,y \rangle
\]

\textbf{Update (Proximal)}
\[
x^* = x_0 + \lambda \bigl(y - \mathrm{softmax}(x^*)\bigr)
\]

\textit{Solved as a proximal operator.}

\section{Local Non-Expansiveness}
\label{sec:Non-Expansiveness}
To validate the local non-expansiveness assumption, we manually calculate this across various disturbances to determine whether the linear layer projection respects the non-expansiveness condition. Provided that the linear layer has a sufficiently large dimensionality, the local non-expansiveness property holds robustly.
